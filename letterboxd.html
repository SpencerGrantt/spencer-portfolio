Case Study

Reframing Letterboxd

Scroll far enough into a Letterboxd comment thread and you'll hit a Petco ad before you hit the reply you were looking for. That's not a metaphor — it's a screenshot I circled in red during the audit that started this project.

Role

Solo — competitive research, review mining, wireframing, hi-fi UI

Timeline

4 weeks, July 2025

Tools

Figma, Notion

The full interactive prototype — click through it directly, or open it in Figma →

A film app with no easy way to find your friends

I use Letterboxd the way a lot of people do — logging what I watch, rating it, occasionally reading a review that's funnier than the movie. What kept nagging at me was how hard the app made it to actually connect with anyone. So instead of guessing at what was broken, I went looking for evidence: I read through 50-plus community reviews and forum threads, and audited five competing apps — Trakt, IMDb, Taste, TV Time, Goodreads — to see how each of them handled the same problems Letterboxd was clearly struggling with.

Three things kept surfacing. Social features existed, but they were scattered across screens with no obvious home — one user put it bluntly: "The app lacks coherence and clarity relating to searching for your friends and anything related to interaction." Navigation patterns shifted depending on which part of the app you were in, so nothing felt learnable. And almost 30% of the reviews I read wanted TV show tracking that simply didn't exist — one commenter had already designed the feature in their head: "TV should be its own entire section of the app... there should be a toggle button that switches the UI to all TV shows until you toggle it back to movies."

Two people, pulling the app in different directions

The research pointed to two distinct ways people actually use Letterboxd. Alex is a film curator — he wants deep engagement: award-based discovery, curated lists, critic-level detail on what he's watching. Jasmine is a social sharer — she's not there for the criticism, she's there to see what her friends watched, reply to a review, and argue about last night's episode without hunting for where that conversation even lives. Neither of them was well served by an app built around a single, undifferentiated activity feed. Any fix had to work for both without turning into two apps.

Three moves, not a full rebuild

I didn't want to design a different app wearing Letterboxd's name — the minimalist, film-first identity is most of why people love it. So the redesign stayed narrow on purpose: three targeted changes, each one answering a specific complaint I'd actually read, rather than a wishlist of features nobody asked for.

Give the social layer an actual home

In the current app, seeing what friends watched and finding people to follow live in different places, neither obvious. The redesign puts a unified activity feed front and center, with an always-visible "Add Friends" action and recent activity sitting at the top of the screen instead of buried under a tab. If the whole point of a social feature is that you actually use it, it can't require three taps to remember where it is.

Let a conversation actually be a conversation

This is where the Petco ad comes back in. In the current app, comment threads are flat, cramped, and interrupted by ad units mid-scroll — the exact moment someone's trying to follow a reply gets derailed by pet food. The redesign nests replies properly, adds inline reactions, timestamps, and filtering, and gets the ad out of the conversation's way. None of this is a flashy feature; it's just the difference between a comment section people use and one they scroll past.

Make a poster carry more information

Small change, real payoff: award badges and age ratings on movie posters mean recognition happens before anyone reads a word. It's a minor visual addition, but it does real work — a user scanning a feed can tell an award-winner from a forgettable rewatch at a glance, which matters more the faster people actually scroll.

And the one people were already asking for

The TV integration users described almost exactly matches what shipped: a toggle in the exploration and activity screens that switches the whole UI between film and TV mode, episode-level reviews, season progress tracking, and separate activity feeds so the two don't clutter each other. It's a genuinely new surface area for the app, which is exactly why it got the toggle treatment instead of being crammed into the existing film-only navigation.

From sketch to shipped-looking screens

Every screen went through three passes before it looked like what's shown above. Low-fidelity wireframes validated the new navigation hierarchy and mapped where the social hub would actually live. Mid-fidelity added the real content — TV progress tracking, nested comments, the toggle placement — and started refining the iconography. High-fidelity is where the visual system landed for good: a dark, near-black base (#14171C) with a cyan-blue accent (#00BFFA) for primary actions and an orange accent (#F27405) reserved for emphasis, set in Proxima Nova. Dark enough to stay out of the way of movie posters — which is really the whole point of a film app's UI — while giving the new social and TV features enough visual weight that they don't read as an afterthought.

What I learned

Everything here is built on secondary research — mined reviews and competitive audits, not moderated usability sessions with real Letterboxd users. That's a real limitation, and I don't think it's honest to present this the same way I'd present a project with direct testing behind it. What it taught me instead was how to be disciplined about that gap: every design decision here traces back to a pattern that showed up repeatedly across reviews or a gap that showed up repeatedly across competitors, not to my own assumption about what would be nice to have. When I couldn't validate a choice against a direct observation, I tried to at least validate it against a repeated one.

The other thing this project sharpened was restraint. It would have been easy to turn "add TV support" into a reason to redesign half the app. Prioritizing the three changes that answered actual, repeated complaints — and leaving the rest of Letterboxd's identity alone — is a harder discipline than it sounds, and probably the most transferable skill this project gave me.
