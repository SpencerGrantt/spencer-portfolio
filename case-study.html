Case Study

A clinical alert dashboard, designed to cut hospitalist override

Physicians override 95% of drug-interaction alerts, across 18,354 medication orders at two hospitals, with no improvement since the Meaningful Use era began. (Bryant et al., Applied Clinical Informatics, 2014)

A note on method: No access to practicing hospitalists for primary research, a gated clinical domain out of reach for a solo junior researcher, so the problem frame is built from 18 peer-reviewed and expert sources instead. Evaluative testing (Useberry, originally built in Maze, plus SUS and NASA-TLX) ran with proxy testers: an initial panel of five narrowed to three RNs, the closest available stand-in for a hospitalist.

Problem

Hospitalists handle the highest volume of daily medication orders in inpatient care, and drug-interaction alerts are supposed to catch what they miss. But every alert renders at identical visual priority: no position, size, or color signal separates a life-threatening interaction from a routine one. Volume alone isn't the failure; making everything look equally urgent trains clinicians to ignore all of it.

Solution

Three things the design needed to answer: the minimum info a hospitalist needs to triage an alert without leaving the alert view, what signal reads as "critical" before any text gets read, and when an AI confidence score actually changes behavior instead of getting overridden. The answer: a triage table with ESI severity tiers already in clinical vocabulary, AI confidence surfaced inline, and friction that scales with risk: one click to accept the recommendation, a reasoned form to override it.

A table, color as backup only

Every alert is a row: severity, drug ordered vs. drug on file, AI confidence, recommended action, all visible without expanding. Strip the color out and the ESI-1 row is still identifiable by position, height, and weight; color-only encoding fails color-deficient clinicians and is unreliable under time pressure regardless.

Mazur et al. 2019; NN/g 2018; The Joint Commission 2013/2016

ESI, not an invented scale

The panel borrows the Emergency Severity Index, already in a hospitalist's vocabulary, instead of a made-up "High/Medium/Low." "ESI-1" needs zero explanation mid-shift.

The Joint Commission 2013/2016; Hussain et al. 2019

Friction scales with risk

The recommended action (Hold) completes in one click. Overriding requires picking a reason from five visible radio options, not a dropdown, before Confirm enables. Dropdown reasons are too coarse; clinicians pick the fastest option, not the accurate one.

Payne et al. 2015

State the AI's recommendation

High-confidence AI predictions get overridden far less (1.7% vs. 99.3% at lower confidence, across 6,689 cardiovascular cases), but only when the confidence score actually connects to the recommended action, not left sitting in a separate evidence panel. Round 1 styled Escalate as secondary with nothing stated; Round 2 made it primary and added a one-line stated recommendation. See Process below for whether it worked.

A schema bug caught before it shipped

Extracting alert fields with the Claude API, the first schema labeled fields drug_primary / drug_interacting. On a Warfarin + Ciprofloxacin case, that reversed the prescribing context. Renamed to drug_ordered / drug_on_file, self-explanatory, no inference required.

Payne et al. 2015

Round 2 (n=2) shows the Round 1 fix didn't move the AI-confidence decision: still 0% chose Escalate, same as Round 1. SUS and override-form completion both moved down, though with only 2 respondents that's a wide-uncertainty read, not a trend. The more useful result was diagnostic, not directional: the two testers reached the same wrong answer in very different ways (11 seconds and 1 click vs. 87 seconds and 3 clicks), pointing at two separate causes: one likely never saw the new recommendation, the other saw it and still didn't act on it, closer to not trusting the AI's confidence score than not noticing it. That reframes the next fix as two problems, not one: a visibility problem and a trust problem. With recruiting for this panel already at two months for three testers, I'm closing testing here and documenting the next iteration as a recommendation rather than running a third round.

What I learned

Nobody chose the right action

Testers didn't converge on the same wrong answer (2 Override, 1 Hold), which killed my first theory that Override was just styled too temptingly. The real gap: the AI's confidence score sat in a separate evidence panel, disconnected from the button row. Different testers, same miss: the interface was the shared cause, not their judgment.

"Completed" isn't "correct"

I first read the testing platform's "completed" status as "chose the right action." They're not the same thing. Re-checking the actual click path showed the tester had made the same wrong choice as Round 1. Caught before publishing by going back to raw session data instead of trusting the dashboard's own labels. Now a standing check for every testing round.

Where this stands

Both rounds are done. Alert triage works: every tester found the critical alert first, nothing to change there. The AI-confidence decision doesn't: two rounds of evidence, still 0% choosing the recommended action, split between a visibility problem and a trust problem. A bigger button only fixes the first. If I kept building this, the next move is a required confirmation step that makes a clinician actively acknowledge the AI's recommendation before overriding a high-confidence critical alert, tested with a moderated session that can ask why someone chose what they chose, and directly probe trust in the AI's confidence score, instead of inferring both from click timestamps. Recruiting this panel took two months for three testers, so that's documented as the next step rather than actually run.
