Case Study

CivicPulse

The bill on CivicPulse's Legislation screen is real. "Housing Options and Opportunity Act (25-0066)" is pulled directly from a Baltimore mayor's-office press release, not invented copy — because a civic app that fakes its data teaches people not to trust civic apps, which is exactly the problem it's supposed to solve.

Role

Solo — research, personas, information architecture, hi-fi UI

Timeline

4 weeks, August 2025

Tools

Figma, Notion

The full interactive prototype — click through it directly, or open it in Figma →

Local government publishes everything and explains nothing

Every city government I looked at technically makes its data public — council votes, budgets, 311 requests, zoning changes. Almost none of it is usable by the person it's supposed to serve. It's buried in PDFs, split across departments that don't cross-reference each other, and written in language built for other city employees, not residents. One resident put the actual problem better than I could: "It's not about information overload — it's about information in the right format." The data existed. The interface to it didn't.

That gap is what CivicPulse is built to close — a mobile-first platform that takes the same public budget, legislation, and meeting data cities already publish and gives it a format an actual resident would use.

An organizer and a bystander, both stuck with the same broken tools

Two people shaped the design. Jasmine runs community programs in Baltimore and already shows up to council meetings and coordinates housing campaigns — she doesn't need to be convinced civic engagement matters, she needs a way to track a bill from proposal to outcome without digging through a different city website for every stage. Jordan is the opposite starting point: he doesn't vote locally, feels a little guilty about it, and picks up what he knows from Instagram and TikTok rather than any city source. He's not looking for a research tool — he wants to know what's happening in his district without deciding to become an activist first.

Designing for both meant the same information had to work at two different depths: skimmable for Jordan, trackable and shareable for Jasmine. Neither version could be the "real" one with the other bolted on.

A dashboard that starts with what you already follow

The home screen isn't a feed of everything happening in city government — it's a starred list of what a specific resident already cares about, plus the meetings and local initiatives tied to their neighborhood. A funding approval on a nearby field house sits next to a housing bill with an upcoming committee vote, both one tap from full detail. The goal was to make "what's relevant to me" the default view instead of something a user has to filter for themselves.

Making a bill's progress visible, and its source checkable

Every bill screen states its status in plain language ("In Committee") next to a one-tap "Track Progress" action, backed by a real source link to the originating government press release. That link matters more than it looks like it should — one of the biggest research findings was that people don't just want civic information simplified, they want to be able to verify it wasn't simplified into something misleading. A neutral, sourced summary builds more trust than a confident one with nothing behind it.

Spending, reorganized around a block instead of a department

City budgets are normally published by department — a format built for city accountants, not someone wondering why their street hasn't been repaved. CivicPulse's Spending tab reorganizes the same underlying budget data around neighborhoods instead: Bridgeview-Greenlawn, Park Heights, Harwood, each with its own estimated allocation, searchable and comparable. Same numbers, reframed around the geography a resident actually lives in rather than the org chart a city runs on.

Meetings you'd actually know about in time to attend

"Lack of timely alerts" came up constantly in research — city meeting schedules exist, but nobody finds out about a hearing that affects them until after it happened. The Meetings screen is a straightforward monthly calendar with one-tap RSVP on anything upcoming, deliberately unglamorous: the design problem here wasn't a clever interaction, it was just surfacing dates that were already public but functionally invisible.

Personalizing by ZIP code, and saying so out loud

Onboarding asks for a ZIP code before anything else, with the reason stated in the same breath — "This data will only be used to track local legislation" — and a Skip option that doesn't punish someone for saying no. Research flagged geo-personalization as one of the fastest ways to lose a user's trust if it isn't handled transparently; asking for location data silently, the way a lot of apps do, was never on the table for something explicitly trying to earn civic trust.

What I learned

Civic tech turned out to be a harder design problem than I expected, and not for the reason I assumed going in. The hard part wasn't information architecture — it was that almost every interesting design decision was also a trust decision. Show a rep's voting record and you risk it reading as a smear. Personalize by neighborhood and you risk it reading as surveillance. Simplify a bill's language and you risk it reading as spin. I couldn't design around those risks by avoiding them; the research made clear that residents wanted exactly that information. The only real response was to be relentlessly neutral in how it's presented — sourced, plainly worded, and never editorializing — and to treat every one of those trust risks as a constraint on the design, not an edge case to handle later.

The other honest limitation: this is unmoderated concept work, not a product validated with actual Baltimore residents. The budget figures, bill status, and meeting data are grounded in real public sources, but I haven't watched a real Jasmine or Jordan use this and get stuck somewhere I didn't predict. That's the next version of this project, not a footnote — a concept this dependent on earning trust needs to be tested with the people whose trust it's asking for.
